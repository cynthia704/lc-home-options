For Athletes

Athlete Mental Performance Coaching

Athlete mental performance coaching at Liberation Coach is 1:1 work with Victor Pierantoni on your response to a sporting situation. You might bring a mistake you keep replaying, a gap between practice and competition or new expectations around your sport. The conversation looks at what happened, the meaning you gave it and what you want to handle differently.

This individual offer is for adults aged 18 and older.

Focus After a Mistake

Describe what happened and where your attention went next. You may want to understand the replay in your head, the meaning you gave the mistake or what you wanted to do in the next moment.

Resilience concerns your response after a setback. If you want more mental toughness, describe what that would mean in a particular moment: returning attention to the next play, staying involved after a difficult result or learning from what happened. Mental skills such as directing attention, setting an intention and noticing self-talk give names to parts of that experience. Naming the concern helps you compare the support you need; any specific training or practice included in coaching needs to be clear before you commit.

When Practice and Competition Feel Different

Bring one example of the difference. What did you notice before, during and after it? You do not need to diagnose the cause to explain the question you want help with.

Confidence can be part of that difference: you may trust a skill in practice and begin doubting it when the result matters. Performance anxiety can involve worry about making a mistake or being judged before or during competition. Describe the moment, what you were thinking and how it affected your attention. These descriptions identify a concern to discuss; they do not establish its cause or diagnose an anxiety disorder.

When the Demands Around Your Sport Change

New expectations, attention and requests from other people can raise questions beyond the next performance. You may want to explore a conversation you need to have, a request you are unsure about or a boundary that matters to you.

When Enjoyment Changes

What do you still value about your sport, and what feels different now? That question can give the conversation a starting point beyond a score or result.

Example: Replaying a Mistake

Imagine an athlete whose attention stays on an earlier mistake while play continues. They could describe the event, the words they kept repeating to themselves and what they missed while thinking about it. “I made a mistake” and “I always let people down” describe different things: one names an event; the other is a conclusion about the person.

A possible coaching question is, “What would you like to notice or do when your attention goes back to that moment?” The answer gives the athlete a specific intention to discuss. It does not establish the cause, prescribe a mental skill or guarantee a better performance.

This is a fictional illustration, not an athlete testimonial or a claim about Victor's results.

One Real Situation Is Enough to Start Describing Your Needs

Explain the moment, what you noticed and what you would like to handle differently. Include what your sport asks of you and the support you already receive. A technical coach, healthcare professional or other specialist may already be helping with a different part of the situation.

Read the coaching approach to understand the approach described on this site, then review Victor's background and the scope of 1:1 coaching. Relevant experience for your sport and circumstances matters when choosing support.

The Whole Situation Matters

Looking at your response does not make you responsible for a teammate's conduct, a coach's decisions or an unsafe environment. Some issues need action from a team or organisation. Technical instruction, injury care and mental health concerns also need the appropriate qualified support; individual coaching does not replace them.
